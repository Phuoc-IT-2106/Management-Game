# GAME VISION

## Product Thesis
A PC business strategy and management simulation in which the player begins by operating a professional esports organization as its CEO / executive, uses competitive success to build reputation, fandom, commercial value, and organizational capability, competes against evolving rival organizations, and may eventually expand the company into multiple sports and business sectors.

The central challenge is not merely to win matches. It is to build, operate, adapt, and protect a growing company inside a competitive and changing world.

## Strategic Center
The game is **company-first, beginning with esports**.

Esports provides the first operating environment and first major validation domain.

Company management provides the main strategic decision space.

Long-term expansion provides future progression potential, but broad expansion must not be implemented before the central management model is proven.

## Player Role and Authority
The player primarily acts as a CEO / executive while remaining directly involved in important management decisions.

### Executive Decisions
Examples include:
- company strategy
- budgets and capital allocation
- organizational priorities
- leadership appointments
- major investments
- expansion
- major partnerships

### Management Decisions
Examples include:
- roster strategy
- key staff hiring
- contracts
- sponsors
- scouting direction
- preparation priorities
- department resource allocation

### Operational Decisions
Detailed daily tasks, repetitive scheduling, and other low-value operational work are secondary.

They should usually be:
- abstracted,
- simplified,
- or delegated

unless they create substantial strategic value.

As the organization grows, delegation should become increasingly important.

## Player Experience
The game should make the player think:

- Where should I invest limited resources?
- Which opportunity is worth the risk?
- Which people or departments deserve attention?
- Should I protect short-term competitive strength or accept a temporary decline for a stronger future position?
- Should I grow now or preserve cash and flexibility?
- Is this sponsor, hire, contract, or partnership strategically useful?
- What information can I trust, and what remains uncertain?
- Which rival organizations threaten my current strategy?
- How much complexity can the company sustain?
- Which problems are temporary, and which require structural change?
- When should I intervene directly, and when should I delegate?

## Core Strategic Relationship
Competitive performance is a major strategic asset rather than the sole definition of success.

A typical reinforcing relationship may look like:

Competitive Performance
→ Reputation
→ Fandom / Audience
→ Commercial Value
→ Revenue & Opportunities
→ Company Growth
→ Greater Competitive Capacity

This relationship must include costs, risks, competition, constraints, and diminishing returns so that success does not become automatic infinite growth.

Short-term competitive performance may sometimes be sacrificed when doing so improves long-term company value, financial stability, future competitive potential, organizational restructuring, expansion capacity, or risk control.

## Competitive Management Vision
Competitive management should support scalable control.

### Direct Control
Players who want deeper involvement may participate in meaningful areas such as:
- opponent analysis
- strategic approach
- meta adaptation
- preparation priorities
- lineup / drafting decisions where relevant
- player roles
- tactical instructions
- selected situational decisions

### Delegated Control
Coaches and competitive staff may analyze, recommend, and make decisions within assigned authority.

The player should be able to:
- accept recommendations
- modify recommendations
- override important decisions
- define strategic boundaries
- delegate most competitive operations

Exact match-simulation depth and tactical granularity remain later specification decisions.

## Simulation Philosophy
Default to **medium simulation depth with selective deep systems**.

Use deeper simulation when additional detail creates:
- meaningful strategic decisions
- emergent behavior
- stronger long-term consequences
- useful uncertainty
- better organizational gameplay

Keep low-value detail abstracted, simplified, or delegatable.

Design rule:

**Deep where decisions matter. Simple where detail becomes repetitive work.**

## Design Pillars

### 1. Strategic Trade-offs
Important decisions should consume, risk, or foreclose something meaningful.

Relevant constraints may include:
- money
- time
- staff capacity
- information
- reputation
- flexibility
- opportunity
- risk tolerance

Good decisions should rarely be universally optimal.

### 2. Interconnected Consequences
Competitive performance, people, money, reputation, audience, sponsors, contracts, risk, and organizational capacity should influence one another.

Avoid isolated mechanics that only modify one number.

### 3. Adaptive Organization
A strategy that works now should not automatically remain optimal forever.

The player should periodically reconsider staffing, spending, contracts, roster strategy, organization structure, and growth priorities as the environment and company state change.

### 4. Evolving Competitive World
The player should operate inside a world containing multiple rival organizations rather than an isolated economy.

Rivals should be able to improve, decline, rebuild, change priorities, compete for talent and sponsors, and otherwise create meaningful external pressure at a simulation depth appropriate to the game.

### 5. Imperfect Information
The player should make informed decisions without complete omniscience.

Information quality may improve through scouting, analytics, staff quality, relationships, market access, and experience.

Uncertainty should create reasoning rather than arbitrary punishment.

### 6. Growth Has a Cost
Growth should create capabilities and opportunities, but also coordination requirements, management overhead, financial exposure, staffing needs, and reputation risk.

A large company should not simply behave like a small company with larger numbers.

### 7. Delegation Enables Scale
As the company grows, the player should be able to transfer operational authority to staff while retaining strategic control.

Delegation quality may depend on the people, information, incentives, authority, and organizational structure involved.

### 8. Emergent Situations Over Scripted Chores
Persistent simulation state should create problems and opportunities.

Randomness may influence circumstances, but it should not replace player reasoning.

Authored events should enrich systemic situations rather than become the sole source of variety.

## Structural Failure Philosophy
Failure should have real strategic weight.

The company may face financial distress, inability to meet obligations, forced downsizing, restructuring, or other structural consequences.

Where practical, serious failure should first create recovery gameplay rather than immediate campaign termination.

Terminal failure may exist when the company can no longer recover from genuine structural insolvency or equivalent loss of viability.

Exact debt, valuation, insolvency, and termination thresholds are intentionally not defined at the vision level.

## Long-Term Company Fantasy
Possible future evolution may include:

Esports Organization
→ Multi-Team Esports Company
→ Sports / Entertainment Group
→ Media / Advertising / Events
→ Multi-Division Strategic Company

This is a direction, not a mandatory linear progression.

Possible future domains may include:
- traditional sports
- media
- advertising
- events
- merchandising
- talent management
- facilities
- partnerships
- investments

Future divisions should reuse shared company concepts where appropriate and introduce domain-specific systems only when they create new strategic decisions.

## Anti-Repetition Principle
Variation should emerge from changing simulation state, not simply from random text events.

Useful sources of variation may include:
- market shifts
- rival strategy
- talent development
- competitive meta
- contracts
- staff turnover
- financial pressure
- sponsor priorities
- reputation
- audience / fandom trends
- organizational capacity
- expansion decisions
- external shocks

The important test is not whether values change.

The important test is whether changing conditions alter the player's reasoning, constraints, opportunities, timing, or trade-offs.

Randomness should create situations to reason about, not replace reasoning.

## Early Scope Boundary
The broad long-term vision must not create broad early implementation.

The first playable proof should remain centered on:
- 1 company
- 1 esports discipline
- 1 primary team
- limited staff
- limited rivals / market
- contracts
- competition
- sponsors / commercial value
- basic finance and financial pressure
- reputation / audience effects where needed
- time progression
- bounded direct / delegated competitive management

Do not require the first product to include multiple industries, a global economy, highly detailed accounting, realistic simulation of every employee, complex 3D presentation, or large procedural content systems.
